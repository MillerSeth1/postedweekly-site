The Small Business Month-End Close: A 10-Step Checklist You Can Finish in an Afternoon

Sample post written for Ledgerwise Bookkeeping, a fictional small-business bookkeeping firm.

Ask a small business owner when they last looked closely at their books and the honest answer is often "when my accountant asked for them." That's how a $40 duplicate charge turns into a $4,000 cleanup project by April.

A month-end close is the habit that prevents it. You confirm that what your books say matches what actually happened in your bank accounts, then lock the month so it can't quietly change. Big companies take days to close. A small business with one bank account, a credit card and a few dozen transactions can usually do it in two to four hours.

Here's the 10-step checklist we use with our own clients. Work through it in order, because each step depends on the one before.

Before you start: gather these

  • Bank and credit card statements for the month, downloaded as PDFs, not just the transaction feed
  • Your payroll provider's summary report for the month
  • Any loan statements, such as equipment loans, a line of credit or an SBA loan
  • Receipts you haven't uploaded yet

Step 1: Record every transaction

Make sure every bank and card transaction for the month has been imported and categorized in your accounting software. Look especially for:

  • Uncategorized or "Ask My Accountant" items. Clear them now, while you still remember what they were.
  • Cash or personal-card purchases made for the business, which won't show up in any feed

Step 2: Reconcile every bank account

Reconciling means matching your books to the bank statement line by line until the ending balances agree. Use the statement ending balance, not today's balance online.

If you're off, the usual causes are:

  • A transaction entered twice, once from the bank feed and once by hand
  • A deposit recorded at the wrong amount, for example net of card processing fees instead of gross
  • A check that hasn't cleared yet. That's normal; it stays on the list as an outstanding item.

Don't "plug" the difference with an adjustment. Find it. An unexplained gap this month becomes two gaps next month.

Step 3: Reconcile every credit card

Same process, using the card statement's closing date. Card statements often close mid-month, so reconcile to the statement period, not the calendar month.

Step 4: Review who owes you (accounts receivable)

Run an A/R aging report. For each invoice over 30 days:

  • Confirm it really is unpaid. Payments sometimes get deposited without being applied to the invoice.
  • Send a reminder, or flag it for a phone call
  • If an invoice will truly never be collected, talk to your bookkeeper about writing it off rather than leaving it to inflate your revenue

Step 5: Review who you owe (accounts payable)

Run an A/P aging report if you track bills. Make sure every vendor bill you received this month is entered, even unpaid ones. Otherwise your profit for the month looks better than it really was.

Step 6: Check payroll and payroll liabilities

Compare the payroll provider's summary to what's in your books:

  • Gross wages, employer taxes and any benefit deductions should match
  • Your payroll liability accounts should return to roughly zero after each tax deposit. A balance that keeps growing usually means the deposits are being categorized as an expense instead of being applied to the liability.

Step 7: Check sales tax, if you collect it

If you sell taxable goods or services, compare the sales tax collected in your books to your sales tax liability account and to what your point-of-sale system reports. Note the filing deadline. Monthly filers in many states are due around the 20th of the following month, but the dates vary by state, so check yours.

Step 8: Record loan payments correctly

A loan payment is not all expense. Only the interest portion is an expense. The principal portion reduces the loan balance on your balance sheet. Use your loan statement to split it. Then compare the loan balance in your books to the lender's statement, which is a quick check that catches years of misclassified payments.

Step 9: Review your profit and loss statement

Now look at the reports, with fresh eyes:

  • Put this month next to last month and the same month last year
  • Any line that jumped or dropped more than about 20% deserves a quick "why?"
  • Look for expenses in odd categories, like fuel under Office Supplies or software under Meals

This is also where the close pays off. You see your real margin while there's still time to act on it, not 11 months later.

Step 10: Lock the month and store your documents

Most accounting software lets you set a closing date with a password, so nobody accidentally edits a finished month. Then save the month's statements and reconciliation reports in a folder named by month.

How long to keep them: the IRS generally says to keep records that support your tax return for at least three years from the filing date, and longer in some situations. Employment tax records should be kept at least four years. IRS Publication 583 covers the details.

The checklist, all in one place

  1. Record and categorize every transaction
  2. Reconcile all bank accounts
  3. Reconcile all credit cards
  4. Review A/R aging and follow up on overdue invoices
  5. Enter all vendor bills and review A/P
  6. Match payroll and clear payroll liabilities
  7. Check sales tax collected against the liability account
  8. Split loan payments into principal and interest
  9. Compare the P&L to last month and last year
  10. Lock the period and file your documents

When it's time to hand it off

If your close regularly takes more than a day, or you keep finding the same reconciliation gaps, it's usually cheaper to hand it to a bookkeeper than to keep spending your evenings on it. Paying a bookkeeper costs less than an expensive tax-season cleanup, and much less than making decisions from wrong numbers.
