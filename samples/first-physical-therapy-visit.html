Your First Physical Therapy Appointment: What Happens and How to Prepare

Sample post written for Northbend Physical Therapy, a fictional outpatient PT clinic.

Most people come to their first physical therapy appointment with a mix of hope and nerves. They wonder whether it will hurt, how long it will take, and whether they need a doctor's note. The first visit is different from every visit after it, so knowing what to expect helps you walk in relaxed and leave with a plan.

Here's what a typical first appointment looks like at an outpatient clinic, and what you can do beforehand to make it count.

Do you need a referral first?

Often, no. Every US state allows some form of direct access, meaning you can see a physical therapist without a physician's referral. But many states attach conditions, such as a limit on visits or days before a referral is required, or limits on certain treatments.

Insurance is a separate question from the law. Some plans require a referral or prior authorization to pay for PT even when your state doesn't. Medicare doesn't require a referral to start, but it does require a physician or other qualified provider to certify your plan of care. Before your visit:

  1. Call the number on your insurance card and ask: "Do I need a referral or authorization for outpatient physical therapy, and how many visits are covered?"
  2. Ask the clinic to verify your benefits as well. Most will do it for free before you arrive.

What to bring

  • Photo ID and insurance card
  • Referral or prescription, if you have one
  • A list of medications, including over-the-counter drugs and supplements
  • Imaging or surgical reports: an MRI or X-ray report, or your operative notes if you had surgery
  • Any brace, splint or device your doctor gave you
  • Your questions, written down. It's easy to forget them once the session starts.

Most clinics send intake forms ahead of time. Filling them out at home saves 15 minutes in the waiting room and gives your therapist time to read your history before you arrive.

What to wear

Your therapist needs to see and move the area being treated, so dress for that.

  • Knee, hip or ankle: shorts, or loose pants that roll up above the knee
  • Shoulder or neck: a tank top or loose T-shirt
  • Back: a comfortable top that can be lifted a little, plus athletic pants
  • Shoes: sneakers you can walk and balance in, not sandals

What happens during the evaluation

The first visit is usually the longest, about 45 to 60 minutes, because it is an evaluation, not just treatment. It generally has four parts.

1. The conversation (10 to 15 minutes)

Your therapist will ask when the problem started and what makes it better or worse. They'll ask what you can't do now that you could before, like climbing stairs, lifting your grandkid or sleeping on your side. Be specific. "It hurts when I reach for the top shelf" is far more useful than "my shoulder hurts."

They will also ask about your goals. This is your chance to say what actually matters to you: running a 5K again, getting through a work shift without pain, or golfing by spring.

2. The physical exam (15 to 20 minutes)

Expect your therapist to measure:

  • Range of motion: how far a joint moves, often measured with a goniometer
  • Strength: using resistance tests
  • Balance and walking, if they're relevant to your problem
  • Special tests that help pinpoint what's causing your symptoms

Some tests may reproduce your pain briefly, and that's useful information. Tell your therapist right away if something is sharp or worse than you expected. They will adjust.

3. A first round of treatment (10 to 20 minutes)

Most people start treatment on day one. That might be a few exercises, hands-on techniques, or advice on how to sleep, sit or lift in the meantime.

4. The plan (5 to 10 minutes)

Before you leave, you should know:

  • What's going on, in plain language
  • How often you'll come, commonly 1 to 3 visits a week, though it varies a lot
  • Roughly how many weeks it may take to reach your goal
  • Your home exercise program

The part most people underestimate: homework

You might spend a couple of hours a week in the clinic. The other 160-plus hours are where most of the change happens. Your home exercise program is usually 2 to 5 exercises, and it's what keeps progress going between visits.

A few tips that help patients stick with it:

  • Ask for the program on paper or in an app, with pictures or videos
  • Tie it to something you already do every day, such as after brushing your teeth or during the evening news
  • Track it on a calendar and bring that to your next visit
  • If an exercise hurts in a way that worries you, stop and call. Don't just skip it until the next appointment.

Will it hurt?

Physical therapy shouldn't be a "no pain, no gain" experience. Mild discomfort or muscle soreness after exercise is common and usually fades within a day or two. Sharp pain, or pain that keeps getting worse, isn't the goal. Your therapist wants to know about it so they can change the plan. Many people find their symptoms start easing within the first few weeks, though recovery after surgery takes longer.

Questions worth asking at your first visit

  1. What do you think is causing my symptoms?
  2. What should I avoid doing right now, and what's safe to keep doing?
  3. How will we measure whether I'm improving?
  4. What should I do if my pain flares up between visits?
  5. When should I expect to notice a difference?

The bottom line

Your first physical therapy appointment is mostly a conversation and an assessment, with a little treatment and a clear plan at the end. Check your insurance, bring your paperwork, dress so the area can be examined, and come ready to describe what you want to get back to doing. That's the fastest way to get there.
