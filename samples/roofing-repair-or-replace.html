Roof Repair or Replacement? 7 Signs That Make the Call for You

Sample post written for Ridgeline Roofing Co., a fictional residential roofing contractor.

A water stain on the ceiling usually leads to one question: can this be patched, or does the whole roof need to go? Homeowners often expect the roofer to push a replacement. Plenty of roofs only need a few hundred dollars of repair, though, and some "repairs" just waste money on a roof that is already worn out.

Here are the seven things our estimators check before recommending either option. You can look at most of them yourself from the ground or the attic before anyone climbs a ladder.

1. How old is the roof?

Age is the first filter. A typical architectural asphalt shingle roof lasts roughly 20 to 30 years, and a 3-tab shingle roof is closer to 15 to 20, depending on ventilation, sun exposure and how it was installed.

  • Under about 12 years old: almost always repair. A leak this early usually points to a specific spot, such as a nail pop, a lifted shingle or a flashing gap.
  • 12 to 18 years: usually repair, unless several other signs below also show up.
  • Within 5 years of the end of its expected life: a repair can buy time, but put the money toward a replacement plan instead of repeated patches.

If you don't know the roof's age, check your home inspection report, permit records or the closing paperwork from when you bought the house.

2. Is the damage in one place or everywhere?

A leak around a single vent pipe or one missing shingle after a windstorm is a localized problem, and repairs are made for it. Widespread damage is different. Curling across a whole slope, cracking on every side of the house, or shingles you can lift with a finger in many places all mean the roofing material itself is failing.

A common rule of thumb in the trade: when damage covers more than about 30% of the roof, replacing the whole thing is usually better value than spot repairs. Patching new shingles into an old roof also rarely matches in color, which matters if you plan to sell.

3. Are there granules in the gutters?

Asphalt shingles are coated with ceramic granules that shield the asphalt from UV light. A handful of granules in the gutters of a new roof is normal, because loose excess comes off after installation. Heavy granule build-up on an older roof, or bald, shiny-black patches on the shingles, means the protective layer is gone. Once that happens, the shingles bake and get brittle quickly. Granule loss across the roof points toward replacement. If it shows up only in one area, look for a hail or foot-traffic cause that may be repairable, and possibly covered by insurance.

4. Does the decking feel soft?

The decking is the plywood or OSB under the shingles. From the attic on a sunny day, look for daylight coming through, dark water stains on the underside of the boards, or sagging between rafters. On the roof, a roofer will notice a spongy feel underfoot.

A small soft area around a known leak can be cut out and re-sheathed during a repair. Soft decking across a large area means moisture has been getting in for a long time. At that point the job is a replacement, and the quote should include a per-sheet price for replacing damaged decking so you aren't surprised mid-job.

5. Is the leak actually coming from the flashing?

A surprising number of "roof leaks" have nothing to do with the shingles. The usual culprits are:

  • Chimney flashing, where the metal step and counter flashing has pulled away or the sealant has cracked
  • Pipe boots, the rubber collars around plumbing vents, which often crack and split from sun exposure long before the shingles wear out
  • Skylight curbs and valleys, where two slopes meet and water is concentrated

All of these are repairable on a roof that is otherwise healthy. If a contractor recommends a full replacement for a leak that traces to a cracked pipe boot on a 9-year-old roof, get a second opinion.

6. How many layers are already on the roof?

Some homes have a newer layer of shingles installed right over an older one, called a "roof-over" or recover. The International Residential Code, which most US jurisdictions adopt in some form, does not allow a recover when two or more layers already exist. Check your local code, but in practice a two-layer roof means the next job is a full tear-off. That affects the cost, since tear-off and disposal add labor and dumpster fees. It also means the decking finally gets inspected.

7. What will it cost over the next 5 years?

This is the question that settles most close calls. Add up:

  1. The repair you need now
  2. Repairs you've paid for in the last 2 to 3 years
  3. The likelihood of more repairs, given the age and the signs above

If you're spending several hundred dollars every year or two on a roof near the end of its life, that money is effectively going into a roof you'll replace anyway. On the other hand, if the roof is mid-life and this is the first problem, a repair is the clear choice.

Also ask about insurance. Sudden damage from hail or wind may be covered. Wear and age generally aren't. A reputable roofer will document storm damage with photos, but they shouldn't promise you a claim will be approved.

A quick decision table

What you see Usually means
One leak, roof under ~15 years Repair
Cracked pipe boot or loose flashing Repair
Storm damage to one slope Repair (check insurance)
Granule loss and curling across several slopes Replace
Soft decking over a large area Replace
Two layers already, and new leaks Replace (tear-off)
Roof near end of life, repeated repairs Replace

What to ask for in an estimate

Whatever we recommend, you should get it in writing with:

  • Photos of the problem areas, so you can see what we saw
  • A line-item price for the repair and, if the roof is borderline, a replacement price so you can compare
  • The shingle brand and line for replacements, the underlayment type and the warranty terms (manufacturer and workmanship are separate)
  • A per-sheet decking price, in case hidden damage turns up

The bottom line

Most roofs tell you what they need if you know where to look: the age, how widespread the damage is, the granules, the decking and the flashing. If your roof is mid-life and the problem is in one spot, repair it and move on. If several signs show up together, a replacement is usually cheaper over five years than chasing leaks.
